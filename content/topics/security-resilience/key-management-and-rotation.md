## Why it matters

Keys wear out: the more data a key protects and the longer it lives, the more exposure. Changing keys on a spacecraft is itself a risky command sequence — if both ends do not switch together, the ground can lock itself out. Operators therefore treat rekeying as a planned, rehearsed activity.

## Plain-language explanation

There are two kinds of keys. Long-lived master keys are used only to protect other keys. Short-lived session keys protect the actual traffic and are replaced regularly by sending new ones up encrypted under a master key (over-the-air rekeying). Every key moves through a lifecycle: prepared, active, retired, destroyed.

## Precise terminology

:::claim{label="standard" source="CCSDS-355.1-B-1" loc="§2.3.2"}
The SDLS key management baseline is a two-tier symmetric scheme: master keys (key-encryption keys) used for management, and session keys (traffic keys) used for SDLS operations; over-the-air rekeying (OTAR) distributes new session keys from the initiator to the recipient, though a mission may fly with preloaded keys only.
:::

:::claim{label="standard" source="CCSDS-355.1-B-1" loc="§2.3.2, figure 2-1"}
The SDLS key lifecycle runs Pre-Activation → Active → Deactivated → Destroyed, through key activation, deactivation and destruction (with key verification available); the Suspended state is not used, and the Compromised state applies only to the initiator.
:::

:::claim{label="standard" source="NIST-800-57" loc="§5.3"}
NIST defines a cryptoperiod as the time a key is authorized for use; a suitable cryptoperiod limits the data available for cryptanalysis, the exposure if a key is compromised, and the time available for attacks, and a compromised key's cryptoperiod is no longer valid.
:::

:::claim{label="standard" source="NIST-800-57" loc="§5.2"}
NIST recommends that a single key be used for only one purpose (for example encryption, integrity authentication or key wrapping), because sharing a key across purposes can weaken both and widens the damage of a compromise.
:::

## Where in the chain

Key management runs as SDLS extended procedures carried over the space link (and in ground key-management systems); the keys themselves are used by the security function on each SA.

## How it works, step by step

1. **Generate and load** master keys before launch; session keys are generated by the initiator (ground).
2. **Upload** new session keys via OTAR, protected by a master key.
3. **Verify and activate** the new key on both ends.
4. **Switch SAs** — create or rekey an SA with the new key, start it, stop the old one.
5. **Deactivate and destroy** old keys at the end of their cryptoperiod.

:::claim{label="standard" source="CCSDS-355.1-B-1" loc="§2.3.3"}
SA management moves an SA from Unkeyed to Keyed with Rekey, to Operational with Start, and back with Stop or Expire; OTAR and on-demand SA creation are optional, for long or complex missions.
:::

## What operators and engineers see

Key and SA status reports from the spacecraft security function, ground key-management logs, and rekey procedures with explicit verification steps and fallbacks.

:::claim{label="example" loc="fictional procedure log"}
`KEY 0x0B upload OK · verify OK · activate OK · SA 0x0012 rekey → keyed · start → operational · SA 0x0011 stop` — fictional; no key material is ever logged.
:::

## Misconceptions and failure symptoms

- **"Rotate keys by deleting the old one first."** Keep the old SA usable until the new one is proven, so commanding is never left without a working SA.
- **"Master keys protect traffic."** They protect keys; traffic uses session keys.
- **"Cryptoperiod is just a calendar date."** It also depends on how much data the key protects and the risk of exposure.
- **Symptom — commanding fails right after a rekey:** ground and spacecraft are on different SAs or key states; use the mission's fallback SA as defined in its procedures.

:::claim{label="mission"}
A "safe rollover" with overlapping SAs and grace periods is a mission design choice, not a single CCSDS procedure (ledger P10). Your mission's key-management plan defines cryptoperiods, rotation schedule, fallbacks and who may authorize each step.
:::

## Worked example

:::claim{label="example" loc="fictional rotation plan"}
A fictional plan rotates the TC session key every 30 days. On day 30: upload new key K2 (OTAR), verify, activate; rekey dormant SA-B with K2 and start it; send a test command on SA-B; only then stop SA-A (K1); deactivate K1 after one more pass; destroy it after the mission's retention period.

**Answer explanation:** at every step at least one operational SA can authenticate commands, so a failure leaves the old path working. Checks happen before the old SA is stopped, not after.
:::
